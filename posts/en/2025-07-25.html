Paper Review: Your Brain on ChatGPT

July 25th, 2025

Introduction

I came across this paper from MIT about how abusing AI tools causes literal brain rot a while back. I thought it was a good time to give it a review. It is quite lengthy, so I will do my best to condense it for you.

AI tools like ChatGPT are more popular than ever. Use them well, and you can experience a boost in productivity. But if you overuse AI, there is a risk of becoming too reliant. The convenience of auto-suggested sentences and summary generation is enticing. Overusing it comes at the expense of your memory, your focus, or your sense of authorship. That's the concern of the study: how humans interact with large language models during essay writing, and what cognitive costs might come with that convenience.

The setup

The authors designed a comprehensive experiment involving 54 participants. They were divided into three groups: one using ChatGPT (the LLM group), another using search engines (but no LLMs), and a final group writing without any tools at all (the Brain-only group). Participants wrote SAT-style essays across three sessions. In the fourth session, the groups were reshuffled: the LLM users had to write unassisted, while the Brain-only group was given access to ChatGPT.

Participants wore EEG headsets to track brain activity during the experiment. Human graders and an NLP tool both scored the essays. The authors interviewed the participants afterward to gather qualitative data about memory, perceived effort, and ownership.

What they found

The results were not subtle. LLM users showed lower brain activity across all relevant EEG frequency bands. This is especially evident in brain regions for attention, memory, and executive function. Their essays also exhibited strong linguistic similarities. There were heavy uses of default phrasings, common n-grams, and repetitive Named Entities.

When asked to quote from their own writing, 83.3% of LLM users failed to do so, and 0% produced an accurate quote. For comparison, both the Search and Brain-only groups exhibit 11.1% failure and over 85% quoting accuracy. Some LLM users could not even recall their main arguments minutes later. Interviews confirmed a low sense of ownership. Only 50% of LLM participants claimed the essay felt like it was theirs.

Brain-only participants showed richer neural activity, including significantly stronger prefrontal and occipito-parietal connectivity. They wrote more distinct essays and remembered what they wrote. They could quote their writings, and reported the highest sense of authorship: up to 89% claimed full ownership. The Search Engine group also outperformed LLM users in most metrics: their essays were more varied, and their engagement was higher. It is likely because the process of synthesizing information still requires active judgment.

During session four, when LLM users were stripped of their tool, their EEG readings stayed low, and their writing performance dipped further. They continued to rely on familiar language patterns from earlier sessions. Meanwhile, the Brain-only participants who were newly introduced to ChatGPT used it more strategically; their EEG data showed increased directed connectivity across all bands, and they retained higher recall and synthesis, suggesting that starting without AI led to more effective and resilient tool use later on.

The concept of cognitive debt

The paper introduces the idea of “cognitive debt,” and it fits well. Like financial debt, cognitive debt builds slowly. You use a shortcut here, lean on a tool there. Eventually, the habit of thinking gets offloaded. At first, you feel more productive. But what you gain in speed, you lose in depth. By the time you are asked to recall, revise, or reflect, the underlying mental structures may no longer be there.

Ownership also takes a hit. LLM users frequently described their essays as “not really mine” or “mostly ChatGPT's.” That sense of detachment is as important as the drop in performance. If students no longer feel like authors of their own work, then what is being learned?

My thoughts: Why this matters

First of all, I want to be clear: this paper does not argue that LLMs are destroying education or that we should all go back to pen and paper. It is not anti-technology. What it is arguing for is intentionality. If we are going to utilize AI in learning environments, then we need to be more deliberate about how and when we use it. Especially in education, the tools students interact with should be designed to keep them thinking, not copying and pasting. That might mean building in pauses, prompts, revision steps, anything that keeps the user mentally engaged rather than sliding into autopilot.

Creativity and critical thinking are part of what makes us human. They are how we make sense of the world, how we adapt, and how we solve new problems. When we allow AI to take over those processes for us, not as a support, but as a substitute, we risk dulling the very abilities we should be sharpening. If you let the AI think for you, you are skipping the part of the work that actually builds understanding. AI is powerful, but if it replaces thought instead of extending it, then it's doing you no favors, and you are misusing a tool that could otherwise be helpful.

Learning is less about arriving at the correct answer, but rather more about how you get there. AI tools might give you a good enough answer, and it might be right, but that answer is not yours. The detours, the confusion, and the synthesis, all of which are important parts of the learning process, can be taken away from you if AI tools are misused. This paper reminds us that the more we offload those parts to a machine, the less we take away for ourselves.
