So, what is a tiling windows manager anyways?

If you've ever used a computer, you're familiar with the usual way windows work: they float around on your screen, overlapping each other like papers scattered on a desk. You click and drag them, resize them manually, and sometimes spend time hunting for that one window that got buried under everything else. A tiling window manager takes a completely different approach. Instead of letting windows pile on top of each other, it automatically arranges them like tiles on a floor, each one fitting neatly next to the others with no wasted space and no overlap.

Think of it like the difference between a messy desk with papers everywhere versus a well-organized filing system where everything has its place. When you open a new window in a tiling setup, it doesn't just plop down wherever. Instead, it gets automatically placed in a logical spot, and all your other windows adjust to make room. The whole system is designed to be controlled primarily with keyboard shortcuts rather than clicking and dragging with your mouse. While this seems complicated at first, learning how to use the computer with exclusively the keyboard can maximize efficiency by minimizing the amount of time you have to use your mouse. Tiling windows managers are especially popular with programmers who want to maximize their screen space and minimize the time spent fiddling with window positions.

What is Niri?

Niri is a relatively new entry in the world of tiling window managers, and it brings a fresh twist to the concept. Instead of organizing your windows in a fixed grid or splitting your screen into static sections, Niri arranges everything along an infinite horizontal strip. Imagine your windows laid out like a filmstrip that extends endlessly to the left and right. You scroll smoothly through this landscape of windows rather than jumping between separate workspaces or screens. What makes Niri particularly interesting is that it's built specifically for modern Linux systems using something called Wayland, which is a newer graphics technology that enables better performance and security. The experience feels fluid and dynamic, with windows gliding into place as you scroll through your workspace. It's written in Rust, a programming language known for being fast and reliable.

Why did I make the switch?

As a programmer and someone who spends a lot of time keeping my hands on the keyboard, having a desktop environment where I can navigate with keystrokes is slightly more efficient than having to drag and drop with the mouse. I have made some decisions to grow as a programmer, and getting used to this way of navigating a computer is part of it. The scrolling features also reduce the complexity. I can spawn a lot of terminals and switch between them efficiently with Niri's virtual desktops and workspaces overview functionalities. Customizability is another selling point of Niri too. By editing its config file, I could edit the hotkey bindings to my personal preferences. I have also customized how the desktop looks with the help of Dank Material Shell. After some tweaks, I have created a desktop environment that is efficient, beautiful, and personal.

Was it worth it?

For the vibes alone? Totally. Dank Material Shell looks a lot better than KDE Plasma in my opinion. In terms of efficiency, I think I'm doing my work about 5% faster. That's not a lot, but considering that I'm still getting used to Niri's keybindings, I could get a lot faster with it the more time I spend working on it.

Want to try out Niri yourself?

One detail to note is that Niri is only available for Linux distributions that use the Wayland display server. Fortunately, it should run on most modern distributions. I am running it on Fedora Linux as a separate graphic session from KDE Plasma. That's the other neat thing too: You don't have to uninstall KDE Plasma or GNOME to try out Niri. If it ends up not working for you, you can always switch back.

If you already are using Linux and want to give Niri a try, you can follow the instructions on their documentation page.
