Studying Abroad: The Gamble Of My Life

February 5th, 2026

Admitting that I got lucky

People often resist acknowledging that success requires good fortune. If you've made it, acknowledging luck feels like diminishing your achievements. If you're still striving, it feels like admitting effort might not matter. But here's what I've learned as an international student in the U.S.: success isn't a matter of skill or luck. It's skill AND luck, working together. Remove either one, and the equation falls apart.

This post is a bit different from my usual posts. As I just completed my academic career and am nearing my start date at Amazon Web Services, I wanted to write something to reflect on how I got here. This post will be mostly personal reflection and my advice to current and prospective international students.

The false binary

We think in extremes: either skills determines everything, or random chance does. Neither is true.

Nikola Tesla was not just brilliant but relentlessly hardworking and ingenious. He worked obsessively, often sleeping only two hours a night, and could visualize entire electrical systems without blueprints. He invented the alternating current system that powers modern civilization. Yet catastrophic bad luck destroyed him: a laboratory fire erased years of work, business partners reneged on royalty agreements that would have made him wealthy, and J.P. Morgan abandoned his projects. The man who gave humanity the electric grid died alone and penniless in a New York hotel room in 1943.

On the flip side, Jack Whittaker won a $315 million Powerball jackpot in 2002. Within a few years, he'd lost most of it through bad decisions and personal tragedies. He had all the luck but lacked the skills to manage it. The same applies to people who get jobs through family connections. Without actual competence, those advantages eventually run out.

Skill and luck work together multiplicatively. If either one is near zero, your outcome is minimal. But when both are present, they amplify each other in ways that neither could achieve alone.

My journey: A series of close calls

Let me be honest about my own path, because I think the details matter more than the generalities. I was lucky to be born into a stable family. I was lucky my parents had the means and foresight to save for my education abroad. That's not something I earned, but it's the foundation I started from, and I'm aware that most people in the world don't get that starting point.

I applied to twenty universities for undergraduate study. COVID prompted many schools to adopt test-optional policies, significantly increasing competitiveness. Out of twenty applications, I got accepted into exactly two schools. Eighteen rejections. If timing had been slightly different, if admissions readers had been in different moods, I might have gotten zero acceptances. I had done everything I could: extracurriculars, AP classes, a strong GPA, and being named Salutatorian. The final outcome came down to factors beyond my control.

Fast forward to internship hunting. The timing was brutal: a struggling economy, an oversaturated Computer Science field, and AI hype making everything uncertain. In 2024, Computer Science majors had some of the highest unemployment rates among recent graduates. I sent out hundreds of applications. Most went into a void.

Then Amazon Web Services picked up my application. That was luck. Passing the interview was skill and luck: I'd prepared extensively, but I was also asked questions that aligned with what I'd practiced. But here's the crucial part: many people who also passed got waitlisted because their teams didn't have headcount. I got matched to a growing team. That was pure organizational chance.

During the internship, I performed well and earned a full-time offer. But even that was partly luck. I was on a team with a budget for conversions while many others faced hiring freezes. I've talked to other interns who performed equally well but didn't get offers because their team's budget got cut. Same company, same performance, different outcome.

And now there's the H-1B visa lottery. It's literally a random number generator deciding whether I can stay in the country where I've built my career. I get three chances because I have a STEM degree and two rolls per lottery because I have a Master's. But "slightly better odds" in a lottery are still fundamentally random. I could have perfect skills and still lose all three rolls and have to leave.

At each stage, I can identify exactly where luck could have failed me. A slight shift in any direction, and I wouldn't be where I am today.

The mindset I try to live by

My approach: control what you can, capitalize on good luck when it comes, and don't dwell on bad luck when it happens.

Controlling what you can means building technical skills, preparing thoroughly for interviews, doing good work once you land an opportunity, networking genuinely, and staying informed about visa policies. These aren't guarantees of success, but they put you in a position to capitalize when opportunities appear.

Capitalizing on good luck means being ready when doors open, even unexpected ones. When AWS called me for that interview, I was ready because I'd been preparing. I've seen people miss chances because they weren't paying attention or were waiting for some "perfect" opportunity. Luck often doesn't look like you expect.

Not dwelling on bad luck is the hardest part. It's easy to say, but much harder when facing an H-1B rejection or a string of interview rejections. The emotional discipline to acknowledge what happened, feel what you need to feel, and then refocus on what comes next takes real practice. Dwelling doesn't change the outcome. It just drains energy you could use to prepare for the next opportunity.

I'm not pretending I have this figured out. But this framework points in the right direction. You're not pretending you control everything, which would be exhausting and delusional. You're also not being passive and fatalistic. You're just playing the hand you're dealt as well as you can.

To students and parents: What I'd want you to know

Here's what I wish more international students and their parents understood going into this process: studying abroad is an investment and a gamble. It may or may not work out. You can do everything right: choose a marketable major, maintain excellent grades, build impressive skills, land internships, and still face forces beyond your control: market downturns, policy changes, timing that puts you in the job market during the worst possible year, and random lottery systems.

The people selling the dream of studying abroad: the admissions consultants, the education agents, and the university recruiters tend to show you the success stories. What they don't show you are the equally talented, equally hardworking students who got unlucky with timing: the ones who graduated into a recession or whose visa applications got caught up in policy changes.

If you frame studying abroad purely as a linear path: get a degree, get a job, get the visa, stay in the country, you're setting yourself up for potential devastation if any link breaks. I've watched people build their entire sense of self-worth around making it in the U.S. or Canada, then completely fall apart when a visa lottery or job offer doesn't go their way.

What might be healthier is investing in skills, growth, and opportunities. I'm going to work hard to maximize my chances. I'm also preparing for multiple scenarios, because I can't control everything. Maybe it works out, and I build a life here. Maybe it doesn't, and I take what I've learned somewhere else. Either way, the experience has value.

Have backup plans. Know what you'll do if the visa lottery doesn't go your way, if the job market in your field crashes, if opportunities exist back home or in other countries. Don't put all your hopes in one uncertain basket. This doesn't mean being pessimistic. It means being realistic that you're taking a calculated risk, and sometimes calculated risks don't pay off even when you did everything right.

Most importantly: don't let bad luck define your worth or your future. If things don't work out despite your best efforts, it doesn't mean you failed. It means you took a shot at something difficult, learned and grew, and the dice didn't land your way. That happens to skilled, hardworking, talented people all the time.

I'm still in the middle of my own story, still waiting on those H-1B lottery rolls. Three chances at a random number generator. I'm preparing for both outcomes, controlling what I can control, trying not to dwell on what I can't.

To make decisions is to gamble

Life is a gamble, and everyone is gambling whether they want to admit it or not. Even people who say they hate gambling are making bets all the time.

Choosing a major is a bet on what the job market will value four years from now. Taking a job is a bet that this opportunity will work out better than the alternatives. Starting a relationship is a bet on compatibility. Moving to a new city is a bet that the opportunities there outweigh what you're leaving behind. Even not deciding is a bet: you're betting that staying put is better than taking the leap.

The people who claim they're not gambling, who talk about "safe" choices and "guaranteed" paths, are just uncomfortable acknowledging inherent uncertainty. But there's no such thing as a safe choice, only different risk profiles. The "safe" corporate job could disappear in a layoff. The "risky" investment could make you wealthy. The "practical" major could become oversaturated. You don't know until after.

So yes, I've been lucky. I was lucky to have parents who saved money for my education. I was lucky to get into those two schools out of twenty applications. I was lucky that Amazon's recruiter pulled my resume from the pile. I was lucky to get matched to a team with headcount when other candidates got waitlisted. All of these could have gone differently through no fault of my own.

But I also prepared. I studied hard enough to be a viable candidate. I practiced technical interviews until I could solve problems under pressure. I executed well when the opportunity came. When luck gave me openings, I had built the skills to walk through them. That's the multiplicative effect. Neither luck nor skill alone would have been enough. I needed both.

That's all any of us can really do. Work on what's in your control, stay ready for opportunities, and when things go well or poorly, keep moving forward either way. I wish you the best of luck in achieving your goals.
